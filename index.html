Summary

  1. System B is not just A minus a constant. In the air, B reads about 4 mph below A with little scatter. On ground balls, B often loses the ball off the bounce and reads 20 to 30 mph low.
  2. Missing data is not random. A drops weak contact: 47% of popups and 14% of ground balls lack an A reading, and those balls are about 15 mph softer. Dropping them inflates A-only averages by about 1.5 mph.
  3. Every ball goes on A's scale: A when available, otherwise a calibrated prediction from B's speed, angle and hit type, otherwise a hit-type imputation of the typical A-missing ball.
  4. A crossed batter + pitcher random-effects model shrinks each batter toward the league mean and removes the quality of pitchers he faced. About 20 batted balls gives a 50% weight to a batter's own data.
  5. Validation: on held-out halves, the model cuts error in estimating true talent from 2.4 mph (raw A mean) to 1.4 mph. It works nearly as well when batters are forced to have only A or only B data.

System A vs System B

The two systems agree closely on balls in the air and disagree badly on balls hit into the ground.

  • In the air (angle above 0): B reads 3 to 4 mph below A, with a spread of about 1 mph. Launch angles agree within about 1 degree. The gap shrinks slightly on popups (about 3 mph).
  • Ground balls: the gap is bimodal. About 48% of ground balls show the normal 4 mph offset; the other 52% read 10 to 40 mph low on B, and B's angle also reads flatter than A's. This looks like B picking up the ball after its first bounce.
  • Correlation: speed 0.82 overall, launch angle 0.97. Nearly all the speed disagreement comes from ground balls.
  • System A's quirk is coverage, not accuracy. A misses 47% of popups and 14% of ground balls. When A is missing, B measures those ground balls at 47 mph vs 71 mph for ground balls A did capture. A drops weak contact.
  • System B's coverage gaps are also skewed: ground balls without a B reading average 75 mph on A, against 86 mph for ground balls B did capture.
  • Hit type U appears on 2 balls; they are treated as line drives for calibration.

Putting every ball on the A scale

System A is the target scale because it agrees with B where B is reliable and stays sane where B fails. Each of the 73,375 balls gets one A-scale speed:

Calibration. A gradient-boosted regression predicts A's speed from B's speed, B's angle and hit type, trained on the 64,943 balls both systems measured. B's angle is the key input: steep negative B angles flag the bounce-tracking failure, so the model knows when to add 25 mph instead of 4. Residual error is about 1.1 mph on fly balls and line drives, 1.4 on popups and 7.0 on ground balls.

Calibration keeps batter signal. For 370 batters with 50+ paired balls, the mean of calibrated-from-B speeds correlates 0.96 with the mean of A speeds, with slope 0.95. Calibrating each ball against league-wide patterns pulls hard hitters only slightly toward the middle.

Why impute rather than drop. A's missing balls are weak contact. Dropping them makes A-only averages too high and penalizes batters whose weak contact A happens to catch. Average speed should include every batted ball, so every ball stays in.

Projection model

Each batter's projection is the league mean plus his estimated batter effect from a crossed random-effects model:

yij = μ + bi + pj + εij,   bi ~ N(0, σb²),  pj ~ N(0, σp²),  εij ~ N(0, σ²)

Here y is a ball's A-scale speed, b is batter i's true talent and p is the effect of pitcher j. Variance components are fit by EM on all 73,375 balls:

  • Shrinkage. A batter's own average gets weight n / (n + 20.3), where 20.3 = 13.6² / 3.0². With 20 balls his data counts half; with 300 it counts about 94%.
  • Pitcher adjustment. Batters facing harder-to-square-up pitchers are credited for it, so the projection is pitcher-neutral. Next season's opponents differ, so a neutral number is the right target.
  • Equal weights. I tested weighting balls by measurement precision. It biased projections up 0.25 mph because the imprecise balls (B-only, imputed) are systematically weak contact. Equal weights fixed it, and the extra calibration noise is small next to 13.6 mph of natural ball-to-ball spread.
  • Next season. Exit velocity is one of the most stable batted-ball skills, so the shrunken true-talent estimate is the projection. No aging curve is applied because ages are not in the data.

Validation

The model estimates true talent to within about 1.4 mph RMSE, against 2.4 mph for a raw system A average. Each batter's balls were split at random into two halves; each method was fit on half 1 and scored on how well it predicted the batter's half-2 average (A scale), weighted by half-2 sample size, across 720 batters. Half-2 averages carry their own sampling noise (3.6 mph² on this weighting), which is subtracted to get error against true talent.

Limitations and next steps

  • Calibration on balls A missed is an extrapolation. It is trained on balls both systems saw, then applied to balls A dropped, which are weaker on average. B's own speed and angle cover most of that shift, but it cannot be checked directly.
  • No game dates, parks or ages. Projections assume one season of data and no aging. With dates, I would weight recent balls more and check year-to-year stability; with ages, I would add an aging curve.
  • Average exit velocity mixes skill and approach. A batter who hits many popups or chops scores low. If the goal is quality of contact, 90th-percentile exit velocity or average on balls in the air would be more stable alternatives worth projecting alongside this one.
  • Hit types are human-coded. A few may be wrong; the calibration also uses B's angle, so it does not lean on hit type alone.
